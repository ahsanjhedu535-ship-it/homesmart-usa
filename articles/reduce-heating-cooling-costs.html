Heating & Cooling

How to Reduce Heating and Cooling Costs at Home

Practical ways to manage thermostat schedules, air leaks, filters, window heat gain, and HVAC maintenance without promising fixed savings.

Heating and cooling costs depend on climate, equipment, energy prices, insulation, air leakage, and the temperatures you choose. The most useful plan begins with comfort and safety, then looks for avoidable heat loss or heat gain. Try manageable changes, measure the result, and reserve major upgrades for a time when you can compare options carefully.

Use a thermostat schedule that fits your household

Choose settings that maintain safe, comfortable conditions while avoiding unnecessary heating or cooling when the home is empty. The best schedule depends on the system and occupants. Heat pumps, radiant systems, and homes with slow response times may need different strategies from conventional furnaces. Follow equipment guidance and consider the needs of infants, older adults, and people sensitive to temperature extremes.

Find and address air leaks

Check for drafts around doors, windows, attic hatches, and plumbing or wiring penetrations. Weatherstripping and suitable caulk can help at appropriate locations. Air sealing should be planned with ventilation and moisture control in mind, especially near combustion appliances. For larger projects, consider a qualified energy assessment to identify leakage and insulation gaps rather than guessing at the most expensive fix.

Maintain filters and airflow

Inspect filters at the frequency stated in the manual and replace them when dirty. Keep return grilles unobstructed and avoid blocking supply vents with furniture. Outdoor units need adequate clearance and should be maintained according to the manufacturer's instructions. Do not remove panels or handle electrical, refrigerant, or combustion components unless you are qualified to do so.

Use fans and window coverings thoughtfully

Ceiling fans can improve perceived comfort when used appropriately, but they do not lower room temperature by themselves; turn them off in empty rooms. In sunny weather, use blinds, shades, or curtains to reduce unwanted heat gain. In cold weather, open coverings when useful sunlight is available and close them when it is not. Window performance and the best strategy vary by climate, orientation, and glazing.

Understand insulation and ventilation

Insulation slows heat transfer, while ventilation manages indoor air and moisture. Both matter. Attics, floors over unconditioned spaces, and walls may have different needs. Before adding insulation or sealing a home tightly, consider moisture pathways, bathroom and kitchen exhaust, and combustion safety. A professional assessment can help prioritize improvements and avoid creating a new indoor-air problem.

Service equipment and plan upgrades

Have equipment inspected according to manufacturer guidance and investigate unusual noises, odors, cycling, or uneven temperatures. When replacing a system, ask for a load calculation and correctly sized equipment rather than relying only on the size of the old unit. Compare efficiency ratings, installed cost, warranty, service availability, and compatibility with your home. An oversized system can create comfort and humidity issues.

Estimate the effect of a hypothetical improvement

The HomeSmart heating and cooling estimator lets you enter a monthly cost and a chosen improvement percentage. It calculates a scenario, not a guaranteed reduction. If you use it, compare the modeled result with your actual bills over similar weather periods. Keep in mind that energy prices, occupancy, and weather can change at the same time as your home improvements.

Know when a major project is justified

Air sealing, insulation, duct repair, equipment replacement, and window upgrades can have different costs and benefits. Ask for an itemized scope, expected performance assumptions, permit needs, and warranty terms. Where available, check official energy-efficiency resources and utility programs for current guidance. Do not assume a particular upgrade always comes first; climate and the existing condition of the home determine priorities.

Frequently asked questions

What thermostat temperature saves the most?

There is no single best setting for every household. Choose safe, comfortable settings and a schedule suited to your system and occupants.

Do ceiling fans cool a room?

They move air and can make people feel cooler, but they do not reduce the room's actual temperature.

Should I replace windows first?

Not automatically. Air sealing, insulation, HVAC issues, shading, and window condition should be assessed together.
